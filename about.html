About the Conference

“Tell me what you eat, I’ll tell you who you are”. --Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin

In “Writing the Food Studies Movement,” Marion Nestle describes the creation of Food Studies as both an intellectual and social movement, as well as an academic field first founded at New York University in 1996, when “food [was] far too common and quotidian to be taken seriously as a field of study, let alone as an agent for social change” (161). Since then, food narratives have been crucial in identifying the profound impact of food on the socio-cultural identity of individuals- reflecting upon the conflicting desires, national and geographical identities, stories of colonial legacies; at times also serving a tool for bringing the communities together. In his essay “Gastro-Politics in the South-Asia”, Arjun Appadurai observes that, “When human beings convert some part of the environment into food, they create a powerful semiotic device. It is therefore a highly condensed social fact”. Over the past few decades, researchers have examined food as a site of memory, identity, indigeneity, cross-cultural communications and “global nationalism”, along with topics related to the production and consumption of food, bringing in readers’ reflections over its metamorphosis due to the sophisticated research across disciplinary domains. Critics have also seen food as something that cannot just be consumed, but also remembered. Being central to the idea of collective belonging, food survives cross-cultural migrations and transnational boundaries through individual, cultural and collective memories, articulated through stories of the edible world. In its more enduring disciplinary areas, researchers have also established the relationship between food as a coded form of connotative language. In food/culinary narratives, the writers articulate their connection with cultural identity, map nostalgia and the conflict of being and belonging, while theorizing meals and recipes dominant to understanding the idea of social and cultural changes, memories of families and communities, traditions and cultural practices, and many more affective aspects of an individual’s life. As a response to the contemporary representations of food in literature and other forms of art, Food Studies has also opened new trajectories for ethnographic studies and historical materialism, as a symbolic explanation for human behavior, among others - focusing upon the interweaving of “aesthetic food and gustatory taste”. It is also fascinating to note that food choices can determine and explore how individuals and communities (re)shape and (re)define their food habits as per the societal and cultural structures, enabling food to be a medium of communication through the language of human behaviors and other systems, under psychological and cultural approaches.

The process from production to consumption of food has also witnessed significant changes over the recent years due to modern agricultural practices and technological advancements. Global concerns over climate changes and environmental effects, shift in food choices under nutrition and dietary practices, challenges in meeting the rising demands of rapidly growing population, and maintaining ecological harmony are some of the factors that have influenced traditional and conventional methods of food production. In modern times, the nurturing and nourishing powers of food are solely dependent on the process and mode of production, ensuring sustainability and addressing social, environmental and cultural challenges and practices. Food industries are focusing on establishing a sustainable process of production through advanced technologies and creating an all-inclusive approach, ‘harmonizing their operations with ecological, social and economic sustainability’. An inquiry into the integration of science and technology, while aligning with sustainable development goals, can merge food science and other technologies for ‘fostering sustainable consumption and production patterns, and addressing climate change, thereby creating a more resilient and equitable food system.

Taking within its purview the above mentioned concerns, this conference aims to bring together researchers and practitioners from Humanities, Sociology, Anthropology, Psychology, Food & Nutrition, Environmental Studies, and other allied disciplines to discuss ideas pertaining to such inter/multi and transdisciplinary approaches towards Food Studies, in a collaborative and inclusive environment. We invite diverse perspectives to break fresh grounds on Food Studies and contribute towards engaging discourses to recognize the complex contexts and concepts in food studies. The event aims at engaging audience in provocative discussions, revolving around significant responses to food studies, rethinking how the journey of food from plate to palate ignites plural perspectives on the role of society, history, cultures and science in (re)shaping Food Studies in a decisive manner.

Objectives

The Conference aims at:
Attempting to substantiate the expanding frontiers of knowledge in food studies, and address the existing gap between Food Studies and other allied disciplines within and outside Humanities.
Deliberating upon the role of cultural and collective memory, and the symbolic and philosophical representations of food.
Provoking scholarly debates on food studies, taking into due cognizance the rising concerns of environment, climate change and sustainability.
Deconstructing the stereotypical representations of food, and conceptualizing it as a guiding factor for the consumers’ behavior.
Considering trans-; cross and multidisciplinary approaches towards conceptual and contextual thinking in food studies.
